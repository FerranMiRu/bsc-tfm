\section*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary}

The AI4Land project at the Barcelona Supercomputing Center trains a deep-learning model to
close a gap in climate data. Global records of how land is used reach back to 1850 and forward
to 2100, but only at a coarse resolution of about thirty kilometres per pixel; the
high-resolution maps that the next generation of climate models needs, at one kilometre per
pixel, exist only for recent decades. The model sharpens the coarse record to one kilometre
across the full time span. Training it on the MareNostrum~5 supercomputer was slow, and a first
measurement showed the graphics processors, the most expensive part of the machine, sitting
idle more than ninety per cent of the time while they waited for data.

This thesis explains why and removes the cause. Working in a loop of profiling a real run,
changing one thing, and measuring the effect, the slow part is traced to how the training data
is stored. The data sits in a very large number of small files on the cluster's shared
filesystem, and the pipeline opens about ninety of them for every training sample. The expense
is not in reading the bytes, which are few, but in the time the filesystem spends locating each
file before any of it can be read. Storing the same data in far fewer, larger files cuts the
opens per sample from about ninety to about nine, while leaving the data the model sees
bit-for-bit identical. A single sample then loads about seven times faster, and a full training
run finishes roughly 4.6 times sooner, with the gain holding as the work is spread across more
processors. The long, periodic stalls that interrupted every earlier run disappear. The limit
on training speed moves off the data pipeline and onto the graphics processors themselves,
which is where it belongs.

The wider result is transferable. For any deep-learning pipeline that reads image patches from
chunked geospatial storage on a supercomputer's shared filesystem, the way those files are laid
out is the single largest factor in how fast it trains. The measurement tools and the layout
principle developed here carry over directly to other projects, including the ELLIOT
foundation-model effort that plans to reuse this pipeline.

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

An Earth System Model, abbreviated ESM throughout the rest of this thesis, is a numerical
simulator that represents the coupled evolution of the atmosphere, the ocean, the sea ice,
the land surface, and the biogeochemical cycles that link them, on a discretized
representation of the planet. The model solves the relevant fluid-dynamic and thermodynamic
equations on a grid that covers the globe, with a finite time step, and it tracks how energy,
water, and carbon move between those components over years, decades, or centuries. ESMs are
the instruments that the climate community uses to produce \emph{climate projections}: given
an assumed trajectory of greenhouse-gas emissions, an ESM predicts how surface temperature,
precipitation, sea level, atmospheric circulation, vegetation, and many other quantities will
evolve over the next century. The projections that inform the assessment reports of the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) are produced by ESMs.

The land surface plays a substantial role in those projections. Vegetation cover, soil
moisture, surface roughness, and human land-management practices regulate how much sunlight
the surface absorbs, how much water evaporates back to the atmosphere, and how much carbon is
exchanged between the biosphere and the atmosphere. The literature has identified land-related
processes as a leading source of uncertainty in the projected terrestrial carbon
cycle~\cite{booth2012carbon,gier2024carbon}: depending on how the land surface is represented
inside the ESM, different models can disagree on whether the land acts as a net carbon sink
or as a net carbon source by the end of the twenty-first century, and that disagreement
propagates into divergent surface-temperature projections.

To represent the land surface in a way that is useful for climate simulation, an ESM needs a
spatially explicit description of \emph{land use} and \emph{land cover}, jointly referred to
by the acronym LULC. Land cover refers to the physical material present at the surface
(forest, grassland, bare soil, urban concrete, water); land use refers to how humans manage
that surface (cropland, pasture, managed forest, urban infrastructure, protected area). The
two concepts overlap but are not identical: a single land-cover class such as ``grassland'' can
be associated with several different land uses, and the management practice often matters more
for the climate than the cover class alone. ESMs ingest LULC information as a \emph{boundary
condition}, that is, as a time-dependent input that the model takes as externally given rather
than predicting on its own.

The most widely used LULC boundary condition for centennial-scale climate simulations is the
Land-Use Harmonization version 2 dataset (LUH2)~\cite{hurtt2020luh2}. LUH2 was produced for
the sixth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6), the coordinated experimental
protocol under which the simulations used by the most recent IPCC assessment were run. LUH2
provides fractional cover for twelve land-use categories on a global grid at a horizontal
resolution of approximately thirty kilometres per pixel, sampled annually from the year 850 to
2100. The future portion of the record is not a single trajectory: it consists of several
alternative continuations, each associated with one of the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways
(SSPs). The SSPs are scenarios constructed by the climate-research community that span
plausible combinations of demographic, economic, technological, and policy assumptions; each
SSP yields a distinct projected evolution of the land surface, which the ESM then ingests as
its land-side boundary condition.

The next generation of ESMs aims to resolve land/atmosphere coupling at horizontal scales of
the order of one kilometre per pixel, fine enough to capture city-scale urban heat islands,
individual agricultural regions, coastal margins, and local hydrological catchments. At that
target resolution, LUH2 is too coarse: a single LUH2 pixel covers an area of roughly nine
hundred square kilometres, larger than many countries' major metropolitan regions and more
than three orders of magnitude coarser, in area, than what the next-generation ESMs require.
Conversely, the high-resolution products that do exist, such as HILDA+~\cite{winkler2020hilda}
(Historic Land Dynamics Assessment, version $+$), provide global land-use maps at
one-kilometre resolution but only for the satellite era, roughly from 1960 onwards in the case
of HILDA+. They contain no record of how the surface looked in 1850 and no projection of how
it will look in 2100.

The mismatch between the temporally complete but spatially coarse LUH2 record and the
spatially fine but temporally limited HILDA+ record is what motivates the AI4Land project, and
will be referred to in the rest of this thesis as the \emph{resolution gap}. Closing the gap
means producing a single dataset, at the high spatial resolution that the next-generation ESMs
demand, over the long temporal window that climate projections require.

The AI4Land project, developed at the Barcelona Supercomputing Center (BSC), addresses the
resolution gap with a \emph{deep learning} model. ``Deep learning'' is the name given to a
family of statistical models, called artificial neural networks, in which a function from an
input vector to an output vector is built up from many layers of simple operations
(typically linear matrix multiplications followed by element-wise nonlinear activation
functions). The free parameters of those operations (the entries of the matrices and the
bias vectors, collectively called the \emph{weights}) are tuned by minimizing a loss function
on a training dataset, using stochastic gradient descent and one of its variants such as
Adam. The minimization process is called \emph{training} the model, and a single full pass
through the training dataset is called an \emph{epoch}. Once a model is trained, it is
\emph{evaluated} on data it has not seen during training (the \emph{test set}) to verify that
it generalizes, that is, that it has learned a function rather than memorized the training
examples.

The specific deep-learning architecture used by AI4Land is a U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet}.
A U-Net is a particular kind of convolutional neural network whose layout resembles the
letter ``U'': the input image is progressively downsampled through a series of convolution and
pooling operations, compressing spatial detail into more abstract features at coarser
resolution (the encoder branch); the resulting compact representation is then progressively
upsampled back to the input resolution, with direct ``skip'' connections that re-inject the
encoder's finer features at each scale (the decoder branch). U-Nets were originally introduced
for biomedical image segmentation and have since become a standard choice for any task that
maps an input image to an output of the same spatial dimensions. AI4Land's task fits that
template: the input is a multi-channel spatial map of climate forcings, and the output is a
spatial map of predicted land-use class labels.

The task itself is framed as \emph{semantic segmentation}. In a semantic segmentation
problem, the model assigns a class label to every pixel of the input image, producing an
output map of the same spatial size as the input but with one categorical channel per pixel
instead of the original input channels. For AI4Land, the per-pixel classes are the consolidated
land-use categories (forest, cropland, pasture, grassland or shrubland, urban, water, other),
and the output is a land-use class for every one-kilometre pixel of the globe at a given
year. The fact that the inputs and the outputs are spatial maps, rather than scalars or
fixed-length vectors, is what makes a U-Net the appropriate choice of architecture.

The inputs that AI4Land fuses go beyond LUH2. Each one-kilometre pixel is described by a
multi-channel feature vector that combines four families of information. The first is the
coarse LUH2 forcing for the year in question, upsampled to the target one-kilometre grid.
The second is a set of static geophysical priors that do not change year to year, including
elevation, slope, and aspect derived from the GEBCO 2024 global elevation and bathymetry
dataset~\cite{gebco2024} (the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans, a community-curated
global ocean-bathymetry and land-elevation grid), and clay, sand, and organic content
extracted from the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory's (PNNL) global one-kilometre soil
parameter dataset~\cite{li2024soil}. The third is a categorical climate-zone descriptor from
the K\"{o}ppen--Geiger classification~\cite{beck2023koppen}, a climatological scheme that
splits the globe into several dozen zones based on temperature and precipitation regimes
(tropical, arid, temperate, continental, polar, and their finer-grained sub-zones). The
fourth is a human-pressure proxy from the ISIMIP2b population dataset~\cite{frieler2017isimip}
(Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project, phase 2b), which provides a global
gridded population record for the historical period and for the same SSP scenarios that LUH2
projects forward.

A further design choice that turns out to be important for the downscaling task is the use of
an \emph{autoregressive prior}. When the model is asked to predict the land-use map for year
$t$, it is also given the model's own predicted map for the preceding year $t-1$ as an
additional input channel. This information is fed through a masking strategy that hides part
of the prior map at random during training, so the model cannot learn to simply copy the
prior at inference time. The autoregressive design propagates temporal coherence forward
across the rollout: the prediction at year $t$ is not independent of the prediction at year
$t-1$, which keeps the downscaled record temporally smooth across a centennial-scale window.
The training methodology, the evaluation protocol, and the global inference results of the
AI4Land model are described in detail in the project's published and submitted
papers~\cite{mozaffari2025neurips,mozaffari2026ai4land}.

The project itself lives inside two Horizon Europe programmes funded by the European
Commission. The first is CONCERTO~\cite{concerto}, a project that works to reduce the
land-driven uncertainty in Earth System Models by improving how the terrestrial carbon cycle is
monitored and modelled, combining Earth observation, data assimilation, and machine learning.
The second is
TerraDT~\cite{terradt}, a programme that constructs a ``digital twin'' of the Earth system
focused on the cryosphere, the land surface, and their interactions; a digital twin in this
context is a high-fidelity, continuously updated simulator of the real system, intended to
support what-if analysis and decision-making. A third Horizon Europe programme,
ELLIOT~\cite{elliot}, plans to adopt the AI4Land training pipeline for training large
geospatial \emph{foundation models}, which are general-purpose deep-learning models trained
once on a very large and heterogeneous corpus of satellite and climate data and then reused
across many downstream prediction tasks. The optimization work in this thesis is therefore
not consumed by AI4Land alone: any improvement in the pipeline propagates immediately to the
training pipeline and tooling that ELLIOT will build on top of it.

Optimizing a training pipeline is not, in itself, a research goal: the science it enables is.
But on a system like MareNostrum~5, allocations are finite, contention with other users is
real, and the cost of a wasted node-hour (measured in money paid for the allocation, in time
spent queueing for it, and in delayed iteration on the science the pipeline supports) is
non-trivial. A pipeline that spends approximately ninety-nine per cent of its time waiting on
data, as the unmodified AI4Land pipeline does during a representative profiling window, is a
pipeline whose investment in H100 graphics processing units (GPUs) is being wasted: the most expensive resource in the
system is sitting idle while the cheaper resources are kept busy. Optimization here serves
three concrete purposes. First, it shortens the research cycle, allowing more independent
experiments per allocation and faster turnaround on each one. Second, it enables the model
to see more of the world in the same wall-clock budget (more spatial coverage, more temporal
range, or both). Third, it produces practical guidelines that are transferable to other
scientific deep-learning pipelines on high-performance computing (HPC) infrastructure, which is
exactly the kind of
contribution the ELLIOT project will reuse.

Optimization in this regime is also closer to art than to science. Bottlenecks shift between
layers as each is addressed, moving from the storage and input/output path to the central
processing unit (CPU), the network, and the GPU's memory and compute units. The
tuning knobs interact non-linearly, so that a change which helps under one set of
conditions can have no effect or even hurt under another. The same change can also yield very
different speedups on different hardware, different datasets, and different numbers of
concurrent jobs. There is no single ``best'' setup that can be read off from first
principles. What works has to be found by experiment, one change at a time, measured
carefully enough that the effect we see can be traced to the change we made.
The methodology this thesis follows (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) is therefore inherently
empirical: profile, hypothesize, change \emph{one} thing, measure, decide.

In short, the goal of this thesis is to make AI4Land training computationally more efficient on
MareNostrum~5: to turn a pipeline that leaves its most expensive hardware idle into one that
keeps that hardware busy. The point is not only to spend a finite allocation better. A pipeline
that trains several times faster also shortens the loop between having an idea and seeing its
result, so the team can run more experiments in the same amount of time and iterate on the
science more often. The next section turns this goal into a small set of specific, measurable
objectives.

% ============================================================
\section{Objectives}
% ============================================================

The main objective of this thesis is to identify and resolve the dominant performance
bottlenecks in the AI4Land training pipeline running on MareNostrum~5. The phrase
\emph{performance bottleneck} refers to the component of the pipeline whose throughput limits
the throughput of the whole. By analogy with the neck of a bottle: removing the constriction
in one place shifts the limiting role to the next-narrowest section, which then becomes the
next target. A useful optimization effort is one in which the bottleneck moves several
times, until the pipeline reaches a point where more work is no longer worth the expected
gain. The concrete sub-objectives that organize the work are the following:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Achieve the fastest possible training time} per epoch on a fixed compute
    budget, while preserving the predictive accuracy of the trained model. The primary
    quantitative measure used throughout the thesis is the per-batch wall-clock time in
    steady state, that is, the number of real seconds each batch takes once training has
    settled into its regular rhythm. We track it together with the full spread of those
    times over a window long enough to capture the tail as well as the median. The tail is what matters here. As the results
    show, the per-batch time is dominated by a small number of long stalls, so it is the tail
    of the distribution, not the median, that sets the epoch wall time.
  \item \textbf{Make it possible to see more of the world within the same wall-clock budget.}
    For AI4Land it means putting more distinct samples through each epoch, so that the model sees
    more of the available place-and-year combinations in the same wall-clock time. The sampling
    scheme that gives this phrase its concrete meaning is described in
    Section~\ref{sec:current-pipeline}. The two paths to it are higher throughput (objective 1) and
    higher hardware utilization, for example by increasing the effective batch size at constant
    epoch time.
  \item \textbf{Quantify each optimization} through profiles produced with NVIDIA Nsight
    Systems and through per-batch timing logs collected outside any profiling window. A
    measured speedup must be tied to the specific component we changed, not to incidental
    factors such as the state of the operating system's metadata cache or contention with
    other users on the cluster, both of which can vary substantially between otherwise
    identical runs.
  \item \textbf{Preserve model accuracy.} Any change to the data pipeline (for example moving
    from multiple Zarr stores to a single store, or applying or skipping an offline
    preprocessing pass) must produce input tensors that are bitwise equivalent to the
    baseline. When bitwise equivalence cannot be guaranteed (for example because of
    floating-point summation order inside a normalization step), the training and validation
    losses must be statistically indistinguishable from those of the baseline over a
    comparable number of batches. A pipeline that is fast but degrades the science is not, in
    this context, an optimized pipeline.
  \item \textbf{Produce reusable guidelines.} The optimizations identified should be
    documented in a form usable by AI4Land contributors, by the ELLIOT project that intends
    to reuse the pipeline, and by users of comparable scientific deep-learning workloads on
    HPC systems more broadly. A speedup that depends on one very specific hardware setup, or
    that breaks when the dataset size changes, is less valuable than a smaller speedup that
    holds across many setups.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Literature Review}

This is an applied optimization problem. There is no single theory that says, in advance, where
a deep-learning data pipeline will be slow on a given machine; the answer depends on the model,
the data layout, the storage hardware, and how many other jobs run at the same time, and it has
to be measured. The useful prior work is practical, and it falls into four threads.

\paragraph{Tuning the data loader.} Practitioner guides for PyTorch's \texttt{DataLoader} agree
on a short list of settings: run several worker processes, pin sample memory so it transfers to
the GPU faster, keep the workers alive between epochs, and let each worker prepare a few batches
ahead~\cite{pytorch_lightning_speed,modexa_dataloader,progressive_robot_dataloader}. The AI4Land
pipeline already did most of this. These settings hide the cost of reading a sample behind other
work, but they do not make the read itself cheaper. One piece of advice does, and it is the one
this thesis pursues: store the data in fewer, larger files.

\paragraph{Reading chunked data into training.} A newer line of work feeds data straight from a
Zarr store into the training loop, rather than first writing out one file per sample, and reports
large speed-ups once the chunk shape matches how the data is actually
read~\cite{earthmover_dataloader,xarray_gpu_pipeline}. These tools mostly target cloud storage or
a direct path into the GPU. AI4Land instead reads from the shared filesystem of a supercomputer,
where, as the next thread shows, the cost is dominated by the act of opening files.

\paragraph{The filesystem as the bottleneck.} On supercomputers, the shared parallel filesystem
is a known weak point for deep learning. It was built to stream a few very large files, while
training asks for huge numbers of small, scattered reads. Reports from large machines show the
filesystem's bookkeeping, the lookups it performs to locate each file, saturating long before the
data itself does~\cite{nextplatform_hpcfs,vef2016gpfs}. The common fixes add a caching layer or
copy the data onto each compute node's local disk~\cite{hvac2022}, and the established practice
for big scientific data is analysis-ready, cloud-optimized storage in sensibly sized chunks,
rather than a dataset split into millions of tiny files~\cite{abernathey2021,dask_chunks}. This thesis takes the portable route and simply cuts the number of
files each sample opens, so no special hardware or caching layer is needed.

\paragraph{AI4Land and downscaling.} The work here extends the AI4Land project directly. Its
papers describe the U-Net, its inputs, and how training spreads across GPUs, reporting
near-perfect scaling from four to thirty-two GPUs and about three hundred samples per second at
the largest size~\cite{mozaffari2025neurips,mozaffari2026ai4land}. What they do not report is
where the time goes inside a single GPU. Scaling well across many GPUs says nothing about whether
each one is kept busy, and the profiles in Section~\ref{sec:results} show that it was not. That
is the gap this thesis fills.

\subsection{Tooling}

The optimization work in this thesis uses a small set of tools that the reader will encounter
repeatedly. Several have been mentioned in passing in the previous subsections; the
descriptions below give each tool its own paragraph and define what it does in enough detail
to make the rest of the document self-contained.

\paragraph{PyTorch~\cite{pytorch}.} PyTorch is an open-source deep-learning library
originally developed by the AI research lab at Meta and now maintained by an
independent foundation. It provides multidimensional arrays called \emph{tensors} that can be
moved transparently between CPU memory and GPU memory; automatic differentiation, meaning the
ability to compute the gradient of any function expressed as a composition of PyTorch
operations without the user writing a derivative by hand; a library of pre-built
neural-network layers (convolutions, linear layers, normalization layers, activation
functions); and an optimizer module that implements gradient-descent variants. AI4Land's
U-Net, its training loop, and its dataloader are all built on PyTorch.

\paragraph{Hugging Face Accelerate~\cite{accelerate}.} Accelerate is a wrapper library on top
of PyTorch developed by Hugging Face, the company best known for hosting the open-source
model and dataset hub of the same name. Accelerate abstracts away the boilerplate that a user
would otherwise have to write to run a training loop on multiple GPUs or multiple nodes: it
handles distributed initialization, gradient synchronization, mixed-precision arithmetic,
gradient accumulation, and the launching of one process per GPU through the
\texttt{accelerate launch} command. The AI4Land training entry point is launched through
Accelerate; the details of the distributed-training scheme it sets up are described in
the Current Training Pipeline subsection below.

\paragraph{Zarr~\cite{newman2024zarr,zarr_software}.} Zarr is a chunked, compressed,
$N$-dimensional array storage format designed for scientific datasets that are too large
to load into memory all at once. A Zarr store consists of a directory tree of small
binary files, one per ``chunk'' of the underlying array, plus a small header file that
records the array's overall shape, data type, chunk layout, and compression scheme. The
reference Python implementation opens a store (on local disk, on a parallel filesystem,
or behind a cloud-bucket endpoint), and slicing the array triggers it to read only the
chunk files that intersect the slice; everything else stays on disk. AI4Land's training
data is stored in this format.

\paragraph{xarray~\cite{xarray}.} Xarray is a Python library that wraps NumPy-style and
Zarr-style arrays with named dimensions and labelled coordinates, in the spirit of the
netCDF file format that the geophysical community has used for decades. Where a raw NumPy
array can only be indexed by integer position, an xarray Dataset can be indexed by the
underlying physical coordinate, for example by year, latitude, and longitude. AI4Land's
data-loading code opens its Zarr stores through xarray and addresses slices by named
coordinate rather than by integer index, which makes the code easier to read but adds a
thin layer of bookkeeping on every access.

\paragraph{Hydra.} Hydra is a configuration management library, also from Meta's
open-source ecosystem, that AI4Land uses to assemble a single training run's configuration
from layered YAML files. A YAML file is a plain-text file that lists settings as indented
name-and-value pairs, meant to be easy for a person to read and edit. The configuration that
Hydra carries sets the patch size, the
batch size, the loss-function weights, the dataset path, and every other runtime parameter
that the rest of this thesis will refer to.

\paragraph{SLURM~\cite{slurm}.} SLURM, the Simple Linux Utility for Resource Management, is
the workload manager that runs on MareNostrum~5 and on most other modern HPC clusters. The
user submits a shell script through the \texttt{sbatch} command, decorated with comments that
SLURM parses as resource requirements: number of nodes, GPUs per node, CPU cores per task,
wall-clock time limit, partition name. SLURM queues the job until matching resources are
free, then allocates them, launches the script on the allocated nodes (one process on the
first node, with the rest reachable through SLURM's \texttt{srun} command), and tears the
allocation down when the script exits.

\paragraph{NVIDIA Nsight Systems~\cite{nsight}.} Nsight Systems is the system-level profiler
used throughout the optimization work in this thesis. A \emph{profiler} is a tool that
records, with timestamps, the events that occur during the execution of a program: function
entries and exits, system calls, GPU kernel launches, memory transfers, blocking primitives.
The profile is replayed visually in an interactive viewer, where each thread of every process,
each GPU stream, and each system-call category appears as a horizontal track on a common time
axis. Looking at the resulting timeline, the analyst can see how the components synchronize
with each other and where any of them spends its time waiting on another. Nsight Systems is
designed for workloads that involve both CPU activity and GPU activity, which is the case
for any deep-learning training job.

\paragraph{NVTX.} The NVIDIA Tools Extension (NVTX) is a lightweight C library, exposed in
PyTorch through a small annotation interface, that lets the application annotate its own
timeline with named ranges. When Nsight Systems
captures a process that uses NVTX, the named ranges appear on the timeline as coloured bars,
making it possible to identify application-specific phases at a glance rather than having to
reverse-engineer them from the raw call stack. The AI4Land training loop is annotated with one
range per phase of a training step (\texttt{dataloader}, \texttt{forward}, \texttt{backward},
\texttt{optimizer\_step}) and one range per step named after its index (\texttt{step\_10},
\texttt{step\_11}, and so on); these are the labels that appear in the profiling results of
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{uv~\cite{uv}.} uv is a Python package and project manager written in Rust by
Astral Software, a company founded in 2022 that builds developer tools for the Python
ecosystem. uv plays the same role that Conda or pip-plus-virtualenv have traditionally
played: it installs Python packages from the public package index, resolves dependency
versions to a mutually compatible set, builds isolated environments, and runs scripts inside
those environments. The qualitative differences are speed (uv's solver and downloader are
roughly an order of magnitude faster than Conda's), determinism (uv produces a lockfile that
pins every transitive dependency to an exact version with hash digests), and interoperability
(uv works on top of the standard \texttt{pyproject.toml} project description, which is the
format the rest of the Python ecosystem also reads). AI4Land's development environment was
migrated to uv as part of this thesis.

\paragraph{ruff~\cite{ruff}.} ruff is a Python linter and code formatter, written in Rust by
the same Astral Software that builds uv. A \emph{linter} is a tool that scans source code
for stylistic problems and likely bugs (unused imports, undefined names, inconsistent
indentation, suspicious patterns that often indicate mistakes); a \emph{formatter} is a tool
that automatically rewrites source code into a canonical style so that all developers
contribute changes in a uniform layout. ruff bundles both roles in a single binary, again
roughly an order of magnitude faster than the historical combination of separate Python tools
it replaces.

\subsection{The Current Training Pipeline}\label{sec:current-pipeline}

Training the U-Net described above consists of repeatedly presenting examples to the model
and adjusting its weights so that its predictions get closer to the corresponding ground-truth
land-use map. A single example is called a \emph{sample}, and consists of a fixed-size
spatial patch of inputs (the multi-channel feature stack described earlier)
paired with the same patch of ground-truth land-use classes drawn from HILDA+. AI4Land uses
patches of $256 \times 256$ pixels, drawn from globally distributed locations chosen by a
Farthest-Point-Sampling procedure that spreads them across the globe. Many samples are
processed together in a \emph{batch}, which is the natural unit of computation on the GPU. The
loss function combines two cross-entropy terms with equal weight ($0.5$ each): an
\emph{overall} term computed over every valid pixel of the sample, and a \emph{change-aware}
term computed only over pixels whose land-use class differs between adjacent years. For each batch, the model produces predictions, computes the combined loss,
back-propagates that loss through the network to compute the gradient with respect to every
weight, and updates the weights by a small step in the direction that reduces the loss. A full
pass through every sample in the training set, one batch at a time, is an epoch.

This sampling scheme is what gives the second objective of the thesis its concrete meaning. The
full training set is every spatial patch at every year in the record, which is far too large to
traverse in a single epoch. Each epoch therefore draws only a subset of it. The
Farthest-Point-Sampling procedure spreads the chosen patches across the whole globe, so every
epoch covers the world spatially, but for any one location only a few of its years are visited
in a given epoch, and a different draw of years is used in the next one. One epoch might show
the model the Barcelona region in 1983, a region of Zimbabwe in 1995, and the Washington area in
2010; the following epoch might show Barcelona in 1962, Zimbabwe in 1987, and Washington in
1974. Over many epochs the model sees a wide range of years at each place, but only a small
slice of them per epoch. ``Seeing more of the world'' therefore means fitting more of these
place-and-year samples into each epoch. Because the training years are fixed by the dataset, the
lever is not a longer time span but a higher number of samples per epoch: a data path that
delivers samples several times faster could let each epoch visit, say, two years per location
instead of one at the same wall-clock cost, so the model sees more of the available combinations
in the same time.

The hardware on which this training runs is MareNostrum~5 (MN5)~\cite{mn5}, the pre-exascale
supercomputer operated by BSC on behalf of the European High-Performance Computing Joint
Undertaking (EuroHPC JU)~\cite{eurohpc}. A supercomputer is a tightly coupled cluster of
hundreds or thousands of compute nodes connected by a high-speed interconnect, designed to
run scientific workloads that do not fit on a single workstation. ``High-Performance
Computing'' is the umbrella term for this class of machine and the workloads run on
them. MareNostrum~5 has several partitions optimized for different workload classes; the one
this thesis targets is the accelerated partition (MN5 ACC), which is built around NVIDIA
H100 graphics processing units. A GPU is a many-core processor originally developed
for real-time graphics rendering and now dominant in deep-learning workloads because its
hardware architecture (thousands of arithmetic units operating in lock-step on vector
operands) is well matched to the dense matrix multiplications at the core of neural-network
training and inference. The H100 is NVIDIA's flagship data-centre GPU released in 2022 and
the workhorse of large-scale AI training in 2024 and 2025. Each MN5 ACC node is equipped
with four H100 GPUs, twenty CPU cores per task, and access to the shared scratch filesystem
of the cluster.

The storage layer on which AI4Land's training data resides is the General Parallel File
System (GPFS), a distributed parallel filesystem that lets many compute nodes read and write
the same files at the same time. GPFS keeps the contents of the files separate from their
\emph{metadata}, the bookkeeping that records each file's name, size, permissions, and where
its bytes physically sit on disk. The metadata is held on dedicated metadata servers. Before a
compute node can read a file, it has to ask one of those servers to look the file up and report
where its bytes are. The time spent on that exchange, travelling to the metadata server,
locating the file, and getting the answer back, is what this thesis calls the
\emph{metadata latency}, or just the latency for short. A single lookup costs on the order of
hundreds of microseconds when nothing else is
competing for the servers, and climbs to several milliseconds when many jobs read at once and
crowd them. Every file the pipeline opens pays this latency once before any of its bytes arrive.
The Results section shows that this per-file latency, not the volume of data moved, is what
governs the cost of loading a training sample.

The training data is stored on the GPFS scratch volume in Zarr format, introduced in the
Tooling subsection above. The AI4Land project uses the term \emph{Analysis-Ready
Cloud-Optimized} (ARCO) to refer to a single Zarr store that fuses all the relevant
variables of a dataset on a common spatial and temporal grid, ready to be consumed by a
training loop without further preprocessing. Section~\ref{sec:results} compares an
ARCO single-store layout against the original five-store layout that the AI4Land team
inherited.

The samples are loaded into the model by a PyTorch~\cite{pytorch} \texttt{DataLoader}.
PyTorch is the deep-learning framework used throughout AI4Land; its \texttt{DataLoader}
component turns a \texttt{Dataset} (a Python object that knows how to produce one sample
given an integer index) into an iterable stream of batches. The \texttt{DataLoader} runs a
configurable number of \emph{worker processes}, each of which fetches samples from disk in
parallel with the main training loop running on the GPU. Each worker also \emph{prefetches}
ahead: it does not wait to be asked for the next sample, but rather starts producing samples
immediately and queues finished samples up to a configurable depth. Two parameters govern this
behaviour. The first is the number of worker processes the loader spawns. The second is the
prefetch factor: how many samples each worker keeps queued ahead of the main loop. Together
they set the largest number of samples that can be ``in flight'' between disk and the GPU at
any time.

To use all four GPUs in a node simultaneously, AI4Land trains under PyTorch's Distributed
Data Parallel (DDP) mechanism. DDP replicates the U-Net on every GPU and partitions the
batches across them: each GPU processes a different slice of the batch, computes a local
gradient, and the local gradients are then averaged across GPUs by a collective operation
called an \emph{all-reduce} before the optimizer step that updates the weights. The result is
that all four GPUs in the node produce a single, consistent set of weight updates, as if the
batch had been processed on a single, four-times-larger GPU. Each of these per-GPU worker
processes is called a \emph{rank}; throughout this thesis one rank corresponds to exactly one
H100 GPU, so a ``four-rank'' run uses four GPUs and an ``eight-rank'' run uses eight GPUs
across two nodes. The orchestration of DDP,
gradient accumulation, and mixed-precision arithmetic is handled by Hugging Face
Accelerate~\cite{accelerate}, the wrapper library introduced in the Tooling subsection above.
The specific mixed-precision format used by AI4Land is \emph{bfloat16} (the \texttt{bf16}
option of Accelerate), a sixteen-bit floating-point format defined by Google and adopted by
NVIDIA's H100 hardware that preserves the dynamic range of the standard 32-bit float (the
same eight-bit exponent) at the cost of trading away precision in the fraction; the practical
effect is that the forward and backward passes of the U-Net consume roughly half the GPU
memory and run noticeably faster than they would in 32-bit, while remaining numerically
stable for the loss values involved.

Jobs are submitted to MN5 through the Simple Linux Utility for Resource Management
(SLURM)~\cite{slurm}, the workload manager introduced in the Tooling subsection above. A
representative single-node AI4Land training configuration uses one task per node on the ACC
partition, where each node provides 80 CPU cores and four H100 GPUs to that task. Accelerate
then spawns one Python process per GPU (so four per node), each effectively using a quarter
of the node's CPU budget, and each process runs its own dataloader with twelve worker
processes (forty-eight workers per node in aggregate). The dataloader is
configured with a batch size of eight (eight samples processed at a time per GPU) and a
prefetch factor of four. Memory pinning is enabled, which speeds up the copy from host memory
to the GPU by holding each sample in a fixed region of host memory that the operating system
will not move. With gradient accumulation over four optimizer-step intervals, this yields an
effective batch size of $4 \times 8 = 32$ samples per weight update on a single GPU and
$4 \times 32 = 128$ samples per weight update across the four GPUs of a node. Under this
configuration, the EuroHPC submission of the AI4Land team reports a single epoch of the
historical model in approximately two hours and forty minutes on one
node~\cite{mozaffari2026ai4land}. The same paper reports near-linear scaling from one to
eight MN5 ACC nodes (four to thirty-two H100 GPUs), with a parallel efficiency of
$97.7\,\%$ at thirty-two GPUs and a sustained throughput of approximately three hundred
samples per second at peak.

\subsection{The U-Net Architecture}

The U-Net used by AI4Land follows the original encoder--decoder design of
Ronneberger et al.~\cite{ronneberger2015unet}, scaled to the inputs and outputs of the
downscaling task. The reference implementation lives in the AI4Land Python package.

The encoder consists of five stages of progressively reduced spatial resolution. The first
stage applies a \emph{double convolution}, that is, two consecutive $3 \times 3$ convolutions
each followed by batch normalization and a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation, which
lifts the multi-channel input into 64 feature channels at the input resolution. The next four
stages each halve the spatial resolution through a $2 \times 2$ max-pooling and double the
channel count, producing feature maps with 128, 256, 512, and 1024 channels at one-half,
one-quarter, one-eighth, and one-sixteenth of the input side length, respectively. The
decoder mirrors the encoder: each of its four stages upsamples its input by a factor of two
(bilinear interpolation with corner alignment), concatenates the result with the corresponding
encoder feature map at the same resolution (the U-Net \emph{skip connection}), and runs
another double convolution. After the four decoder stages the network has returned to the
input resolution with 64 feature channels. A final $1 \times 1$ convolution maps those 64
channels to one logit per land-use class, producing an output map of class scores at the
input resolution. Across the entire encoder--decoder, all internal convolutions use
$3 \times 3$ kernels with padding one (so spatial dimensions are preserved within a stage),
and the only spatial reduction is the max-pooling between stages.

Two of the inputs are categorical rather than continuous: the K\"{o}ppen--Geiger climate zone
and the autoregressive HILDA+ prior. Both are turned into dense feature maps through learned
embedding layers (the same mechanism that turns word tokens into vectors in natural-language
models). The K\"{o}ppen--Geiger embedding has dimension five (the 31 zone classes plus a
padding row); the HILDA+ embedding has dimension two (the 8 land-use classes plus a padding
row that the masking strategy uses). The continuous LUH2 channels, the static geophysical
priors, and the two categorical embeddings are concatenated along the channel axis before
being fed into the first double convolution.

For multi-year prediction, the U-Net is wrapped in a recurrent rollout module that unrolls
the same network autoregressively. At year $t$, the model's own prediction at $t-1$ is
reused as the HILDA+ prior for the next forward pass; the same weights are applied at every
step (the network is recurrent in time even though, as a U-Net, it is feed-forward in
space). During training a \emph{teacher forcing} schedule linearly decays the probability
of using the ground-truth HILDA+ map (rather than the model's own previous prediction) from
one to zero over the first 5\,000 optimizer steps, which gradually exposes the network to
its own errors without destabilizing the early phase of training.

\subsection{Optimization Loop and Measurement}

Optimizing a pipeline like this is empirical work, closer to art than to science. As one
bottleneck is removed the next one appears, and the settings interact, so the best setup cannot
be worked out on paper. The method is therefore a loop. We profile a real training run to see
where the time goes, make one concrete guess about the slowest part, change a single thing to
test that guess, and compare the result against the unchanged baseline. A guess that turns out
wrong is still useful, because it rules a cause out. The plan was to fix the data loader first
and then turn to the GPU. So the loop runs first against the data side, until the data loader can
feed samples as fast as the GPU trains on them. Past that point the data is no longer the limit
and the GPU sets the pace; the same loop then carries on against the compute side, which is where
the work continues beyond this thesis.

This staged way of working has a precedent in Earth System modelling. A performance-analysis
framework from the Barcelona Supercomputing Center follows the same path on climate models:
measure how fast a model can run, find what stops it scaling, then study why~\cite{tinto2019mpi}.
That work also states the case behind this thesis, that cheap analysis at small scale points to
fixes which pay off in the large runs, and that time saved on computation is returned to the
science as more experiments, finer resolution, or larger ensembles.

Two habits keep each comparison sound. We change one thing at a time, so any difference in
timing has a single possible cause. And before timing any change to the data layout, we check
that it still hands the model exactly the same numbers as before, so that a ``faster'' pipeline
is never just one that quietly feeds the model different data.

The harder problem is that MareNostrum~5 is a shared machine. Hundreds of other jobs read and
write the same filesystem at the same time, and how quickly a file is read also depends on
whether it was read recently and is still held in memory. Both of these change from one run to
the next, and neither is under our control. A single timing can therefore mislead: the same
setup can look fast on a quiet filesystem and slow a minute later when a neighbour floods it. To
guard against this, every configuration is measured several times rather than once. We discard
the fastest and the slowest reading of each set and average the rest, so that one unlucky spike
or one lucky fast read does not decide the reported number.

\subsection{Establishing a Reproducible Workflow}

When you optimize a pipeline, you need to iterate quickly on both the source code and the
project dependencies. Changes must not disturb the work of colleagues, who may need a different
set of dependencies. And any experiment must be reproducible on another machine at a later date.
The AI4Land workflow, as inherited, did not offer these properties. The first task of the
optimization work was therefore to improve the code and the surrounding tools so that it did.

\subsubsection{Conda limitations and the move to uv}

The AI4Land project's environment was managed by Conda. Conda is a Python package and environment
manager originally developed for the scientific computing community. It builds isolated
environments, each with its own pinned Python and packages, and resolves dependencies through a
satisfiability solver that searches for a mutually compatible set of versions across the
user-requested packages and their transitive dependencies. Unlike pip, Conda also installs
non-Python system libraries: the linear-algebra backend that NumPy links against, or the Compute
Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) toolkit that PyTorch builds against. That made it well suited,
historically, to scientific work where Python wrappers and native libraries had to match. Since
uv became stable in 2024, many scientific teams have moved away from Conda for projects of
AI4Land's shape; the migration described here follows that broader trend.

Three problems stood in the way of the workflow the optimization work needed.

The first problem is storage. Conda environments are stored on disk as a full copy of every
installed package, with no sharing of files between environments. A modern PyTorch-with-CUDA
environment is already several gigabytes, and the AI4Land environment, with its scientific and
geospatial dependencies layered on top, is correspondingly large. On a shared filesystem with
per-user quotas, holding one environment per development branch was not feasible. The team had
therefore settled on a single environment shared across all branches. The two further problems
below are direct consequences of that decision.

The second problem is that, because the environment was shared, any change to a dependency on one
branch became active for everyone on the same login node. Rolling back to reproduce an earlier
result was not a self-contained operation: the change had to be undone globally and then redone
after the reproduction. On top of that, because Conda installs system libraries alongside Python
packages, the same native library can be brought in by several Conda packages at different
versions, and which version ends up active is decided by the solver's resolution order rather
than by an explicit choice in the project's configuration.

The third problem is that Conda's solver itself is slow. A clean AI4Land environment takes three
to ten minutes to build on the MareNostrum~5 login nodes. Every time a developer needed a
different dependency set, they paid that rebuild cost before they could run an experiment.

uv~\cite{uv} is the Python package and project manager introduced in the Tooling subsection of
the Methodology. Its design addresses each of the three problems above.

The first piece is the shared package cache. uv stores every downloaded package once on disk, in
a content-addressed cache, and hard-links from each environment back into that cache. When two
environments use the same package at the same version, the underlying files are shared instead
of duplicated. The disk cost of a per-branch environment is therefore the cost of the
differences from the cache, not the cost of a full copy. Each developer can keep one environment
per branch instead of sharing a single global one. The second piece is the lockfile. uv writes
out a lockfile that pins every direct and transitive dependency to an exact version, together
with a cryptographic hash. The lockfile is committed into the project; any other developer, or
the same developer on a different machine, reads that file and builds the same environment in
the same way. The third piece is speed. uv's solver and downloader are written in Rust and run
in parallel at every step where parallelism is available; a clean environment that took three to
ten minutes under Conda now takes a few seconds under uv.

The migration itself was carried out in three steps. The first step was to write a
\texttt{pyproject.toml} file, the standard project-description file adopted by the Python
community. The file lists the project's direct dependencies and the version range accepted for
each. The new file pins the project's runtime dependencies, including the CUDA-matched PyTorch
build that the H100 partition requires. The second step was to reorganize the source tree into a
packaged layout, with every AI4Land module under a single source directory. That turns the
codebase into an installable Python package, which means it can be installed once on a node and
used from any directory under SLURM rather than being executed by relative path from the
checkout. The third step was to rewrite the entry-point scripts so that they import from the
installed package rather than from neighbouring files.

After the migration, the three problems disappeared together. Clean environments build in
seconds. The lockfile pins every dependency, so the same environment can be rebuilt on a
different machine identically. Each branch can keep its own environment cheaply, so a code
change on one branch no longer interferes with another branch's environment.

\subsubsection{Code style and tests}

ruff~\cite{ruff} is the Python linter and code formatter introduced in the Tooling subsection.
The reason to adopt it is to keep code review focused on what actually changed and improve code
readability by standardizing the style.

At the granularity at which the optimization work in this thesis operates, where a branch usually
differs from the baseline by a single change, a diff that mixes the substantive change with
unrelated reformatting is harder to read and slower to review. A diff that contains only the
substantive change can be read at a glance.

Having a similar style across the codebase makes it much easier to understand it as a whole and
pinpoint possible bugs. The reader learns what certain constructs look like and what they do, making
it easier to recognize them in a different context. A consistent style also makes it easier to
spot outliers, which are often bugs.

ruff is enforced through a \emph{pre-commit hook}. A pre-commit hook is a small script that
runs automatically before every recorded change to the codebase, and that blocks the change if
the formatter or the linter reports an issue. The hook ensures that the project's style is the
only one that is ever committed. Running ruff on the entire AI4Land codebase takes well under a
second, so the hook does not slow developers down.

The last piece of the workflow change is two small test scripts that run inside SLURM and act
as gates before any change is merged.

The first is a \emph{smoke test}. The smoke test runs a short training epoch end to end on one
H100 GPU and checks that the loss decreases and that no exception is raised. It catches gross
regressions: a refactor that breaks the dataloader, a configuration change that silently
disables an input source, a tensor-shape mismatch introduced by a layout change. The smoke
test finishes in under five minutes.

The second is a \emph{parity test}. The parity test draws a fixed set of samples from two
pipeline variants and checks that the resulting input tensors are bit-identical (or, when
floating-point summation order makes bit-equality impossible, that all per-sample statistics
agree within a strict numerical tolerance). The parity test runs before any performance
comparison between two layouts. A layout change that produces a different tensor is not a valid
performance comparison, because the model would then be training on different data. Every
layout change in this thesis was admitted only after the parity test passed.

\subsubsection{Scientific and workflow impact}

Together, these changes give the rest of the chapter what it needs. Branches build in seconds
and do not interfere with each other, so experiments can be iterated quickly. The lockfile and
the parity tests together pin both the software state and the data state of any reported
measurement, so a result reported in this thesis can be reproduced on a different machine and at
a later date. Code review focuses on the change being reviewed, because formatting noise is
filtered out by ruff at commit time.

The payoff is not only practical but scientific. A pipeline that anyone can rebuild from a
pinned lockfile and install as a package, rather than one that runs only inside a single
hand-maintained environment, is far more likely to be picked up and reused by other groups. The
literature on reproducible and reusable research software makes this case directly: software
that others can install, rerun, and build on is more readily reused, and reproducibility is
widely held to be a precondition for others to trust and extend a computational
result~\cite{sandve2013,barker2022}. The migration described here is therefore also an
investment in the reuse of the AI4Land pipeline, by the ELLIOT project and by any other group
that wants to train on the same data.

\subsection{Initial Profiling}

Before changing anything in the pipeline, we needed to know where the time was actually
going. The first measurement was therefore a profiling run against the AI4Land pipeline as
the team had been running it, with no changes other than the instrumentation needed to record
what each component was doing and when.

The instrumentation has two layers. The lower layer is NVIDIA Nsight Systems, the system
profiler introduced in the Tooling subsection. Nsight Systems records, with timestamps, every
function entry and exit, every system call, every GPU kernel launch, every memory transfer,
and every operation in which one component of the program waits on another. The recorded
events are replayed in an interactive viewer as a visual timeline, with one track per
process, per GPU stream, and per system-call category. The upper layer is NVTX, the
annotation interface also introduced earlier. The training loop is wrapped with named NVTX
ranges (\texttt{dataloader}, \texttt{forward}, \texttt{backward},
\texttt{optimizer\_step}), and each individual training step is also wrapped in a range
named after its index (\texttt{step\_10}, \texttt{step\_11}, and so on). On the Nsight
timeline these ranges appear as coloured bars, which lets us identify at a glance both the
high-level phase (which part of the training loop is running) and the exact step it belongs
to, instead of having to read the raw call stack.

The profiling target was the production training loop on the multi-zarr layout, the data
arrangement that the AI4Land team had been training on. In that layout each input source
lives in its own Zarr store, so the dataloader fetches each sample by reading from five
separate stores in turn. A representative Nsight Systems trace from this configuration is
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:nsys-multi-zarr}. At the very top of the trace is the GPU activity
track. It is essentially idle through the whole window, with brief activity only at the left
and right edges, where the forward, backward, and optimizer-step phases of the neighbouring
training steps fall inside the recorded window. The middle of the trace is occupied by the
NVTX ranges that name what the program is doing. The range that dominates the window is the
\texttt{dataloader} phase of \texttt{step\_11}, which alone takes about seventy-six seconds;
the labels for the neighbouring steps are pushed to the extreme left and right of the window
and are mostly hidden. At the bottom of the trace are the dataloader worker processes, each
spending most of its recorded time blocked in \texttt{sem\_wait}, the system call that
suspends a thread until a semaphore is signalled. The pipeline is bound by data preparation on
the CPU, and the most expensive resource in the system, the H100 GPU, sits idle while the
dataloader workers do their work.

On top of the Nsight trace, the training loop also writes a wall-clock log of how long each
batch takes. Reading that log shows a clear periodic pattern. About twelve consecutive
batches finish in well under a second each, then one batch takes around eighty seconds, then
twelve more fast batches, then another slow batch, and so on. The period of twelve is not a
coincidence: the dataloader runs twelve worker processes, and they never get the chance to build
up the queue of prefetched samples the configuration asks for. The twelve workers each deliver
one sample, the GPU drains those twelve in about a second, and the workers have not finished
fetching the next round before the queue runs dry and the GPU is asking for more. The slow batch
is the main process blocked while all twelve workers open files and refill from disk at once; the
fast batches are those twelve already-prepared samples being consumed. The configured prefetch
cannot hide the stall, because one refill takes far longer than the GPU needs to burn through
everything the workers can stage ahead of it. The cadence is tied to the number of workers: with
eight workers the slow batch would fall on every eighth batch and with twenty on every twentieth,
so the worker count sets how often the stall is paid, a relationship the cost estimates later in
this chapter rely on.

The Nsight trace has a third observation that points at where to look next. Each dataloader
worker thread spends almost all of its recorded time blocked in a low-level operation that
the operating system reports as a \emph{kernel wait}, a state in which the thread is
suspended by the kernel until something releases it. The kernel-wait signature is the same
in two very different situations. It can mean ``the worker is idle, waiting for the main
process to ask for the next sample''. It can also mean ``the worker is busy inside its
per-sample fetch but blocked inside a library call that itself uses a kernel wait at a lower
level''. The Nsight trace on its own cannot say which of the two is happening, because it
records that a worker thread was blocked but not which call inside the worker caused the
block. The trace therefore frames the next question rather than answering it. Either the
inter-process queue between the workers and the main process is the limiter, or some library
inside the per-sample fetch is.

One quantity sets the scale for everything that follows. In this window the pipeline feeds the
GPU far more slowly than the hardware allows: the bytes each sample needs are small, yet they
arrive roughly two hundred times slower than the filesystem can stream data, a gap measured
directly later in this chapter (Section~\ref{sec:bandwidth-ceiling}). A shortfall that large
cannot be blamed on the volume of data. It points instead at a fixed cost paid once per read,
however few bytes that read returns, so the lever that matters is how many separate files a
sample has to open. That number is inflated by the way the data is stored: the inputs are
split across five separate Zarr stores, and every sample opens all five. The first and
simplest step is therefore structural: collapse those five stores into one, so each worker
opens one store per refill instead of five. The change leaves the libraries inside the
per-sample fetch untouched and gives a cleaner baseline against which library-level hypotheses
can be tested in the subsections after it.

\subsection{From Multiple Stores to a Single Store}

The AI4Land team had been training on the \emph{multi-zarr} layout, in which the training
inputs are split across five separate Zarr stores, one per input source. The five sources are
the LUH2 dynamic forcings, the HILDA+ target, the static geophysical priors (elevation,
slope, aspect, soil composition), the K\"{o}ppen--Geiger climate zones, and the ISIMIP2b
population. Every worker, on every sample, opens all five stores, reads the relevant spatial
and temporal slice from each, and assembles the per-sample input tensor by aligning the five
slices on a common grid.

The hypothesis that motivates collapsing the five stores into one is plain. Opening five
stores per worker reads five sets of headers, traverses five directory trees, and acquires
five sets of file handles, whereas opening a single combined store does that work once.
Bundling AI4Land's variables into a single analysis-ready store on a common spatial and
temporal grid should remove the per-sample fixed cost of the four extra stores and produce a
more coherent access pattern on the parallel filesystem. The change is structural, not
algorithmic: the model and the training code do not have to change. It was the first lever
we tried, and the simple expectation behind it is that fewer stores mean less per-sample work.
As the measurements below show, bundling does help, and substantially. But the same runs also
turn up a result the simple expectation does not predict and cannot explain, an early sign that
the number of stores is not the whole story. That thread is picked up once the bundling numbers
are in, and runs through the rest of the chapter.

The bundling was implemented by writing all five input sources out into a single new Zarr
store. Two variants are used throughout the rest of this chapter
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}), both covering a shorter span than the multi-zarr layout. The first
covers the historical window 1960--2015 at the full resolution and is 213 gigabytes on disk.
The second covers a shorter window, 2000--2015, and is 66 gigabytes; the smaller variant
exists to make per-sample behaviour easier to interpret in isolation. Both single-zarr
variants serve bit-identical input tensors to the dataloader (verified through the parity
test described in the previous subsection).

Section~\ref{sec:store-size}, once the diagnostic tools are in place: what is the per-sample
cost actually made of, and why does it grow with the size of a store the fetch barely
touches?

\subsection{Removing the Synchronizer}

With the single-zarr layout established as the new baseline, the next thing to check was
whether the residual kernel waits inside each worker's fetch were caused by a specific
library or by the inter-process queue between workers and the main process. The most
natural library to suspect first was Zarr itself. The reference Python implementation of
Zarr supports concurrent access to a store across multiple processes through a
\emph{process synchronizer}. The process synchronizer is a small object that holds a
file-based lock and that serialises operations across processes through it. Acquiring such
a lock goes through the standard Unix file-locking interface, which uses a kernel wait at
the lowest level, and therefore shows up in profiler traces with the same signature as the
worker stalls we were trying to explain. The synchronizer was enabled on every Zarr store
in the AI4Land code, as a precaution against multiple workers writing concurrently and
corrupting the data. A reasonable first move was therefore to disable it and measure again.

Three independent comparisons were carried out on the single-zarr layout
(Table~\ref{tab:synch}). Inside the production training loop, the per-batch wall-clock
pattern is the same with the synchronizer enabled and disabled: same fast-batch baseline,
same slow batches at the same cadence. In a standalone, single-thread per-sample fetch
(the standalone profiler is described in the next subsection), the mean per-sample time is
essentially identical with the synchronizer enabled and with it disabled, within run-to-run
noise. Inside the Nsight trace, the share of time attributed to kernel waits is essentially
the same in the two cases.

Reading the source of the Zarr library closes the question. In the version of Zarr in use,
the synchronizer's lock is only acquired on the chunk-write path. The chunk-read path,
which is the only path the dataloader exercises, never acquires the lock and therefore
never contends with anything that does. On a read-only workload the synchronizer is simply
not engaged, regardless of whether it is enabled in the configuration.

Two consequences follow. The configuration switch that toggled the synchronizer was removed
from the AI4Land code, since it had no effect on the read path. The synchronizer instance
itself was kept in place defensively, in case a concurrent writer is ever introduced. More
importantly for the rest of the chapter, the kernel-wait pattern is real but its cause is
not the synchronizer. It has to come from somewhere else: from the inter-process queue
used by the PyTorch DataLoader, from the parallel filesystem's client daemon, or from some
library called inside the per-sample fetch. The investigation continues.

\subsection{Isolating the Dataloader}

The measurements collected so far were taken with the full training loop active, so every
per-batch number reflects both the dataloader's work and the model's forward and backward
passes. Attributing a measured speed-up to the dataloader, rather than to incidental
fluctuations on the model side, requires isolating the dataloader.

To do that, a standalone dataloader profiler was developed. The profiler runs the AI4Land
dataset code without instantiating the model or the optimiser; it executes a controlled
sequence of per-sample fetches and records timestamps at every interesting boundary. Each
measurement runs inside a fresh subprocess, so the child cannot inherit any configuration
state that might bias the timing. The profiler is organised into three phases that
progressively widen the measurement scope.

\paragraph{Phase 1: time spent in each input source.} Fifty samples are fetched in sequence
with no worker processes and no concurrency. Inside each fetch, the time spent reading each
input source is recorded separately. The total per-sample fetch in this configuration is
approximately 1.25 seconds. The breakdown across sources is heavily uneven
(Table~\ref{tab:per-source}). A single LUH2 read takes around 428 milliseconds on average,
and because every sample reads LUH2 at two timesteps it accounts for about 68 per cent of
the per-sample fetch. The other sources together account for less than a third of the time. The output tensor that arrives at the model is 9.5 megabytes per sample, so the
effective throughput of one single-threaded reader is around 8 megabytes per second, far
below the parallel filesystem's single-stream bandwidth (revisited in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:bandwidth-ceiling}).

approximately 92 per cent of the per-sample fetch and the preprocess step for approximately
8 per cent. This caps any optimization aimed at the preprocess step at 8 per cent of the
per-sample cost; the load step is where the time is.

At this point the natural follow-up was whether the preprocess cost could be moved offline.
Concretely, the team had been considering writing out a second copy of the dataset with the
fill-NaN and normalize steps already applied to the on-disk bytes, so that the dataloader
would not have to perform them at read time. The hope was that this would eliminate the
dataloader bottleneck. Phase 2 made it possible to check the hope before the dataset was
ever rewritten: a preprocessed variant of the same store was measured against the
non-preprocessed variant under otherwise identical conditions, and the two returned
indistinguishable per-sample fetch times and indistinguishable end-to-end throughput. The
preprocess work that the bake-in would have removed (fill-NaN and normalize) was already
only a small fraction of the cost. The residual preprocess time that remains in both layouts
is the in-memory stacking, transposing, and reshaping, which an offline bake-in cannot
remove. The preprocessed-store idea was therefore not the answer to the dataloader
bottleneck and was not pursued further.

Three of the candidate causes from the initial profiling are ruled out by these three

limit then adding workers would not help; Phase 3 shows that adding workers does help,
linearly. The cost is not in the preprocess step, because Phase 2 caps that contribution at
8 per cent and the preprocessed-store check above reproduces the same numbers. The cost is
concentrated in the load step of each input source, with LUH2 being the largest. The next subsection
drills into what a single chunk read on that path is actually doing.

\subsection{Diagnosing the Bottleneck on Synthetic Data}

\subsubsection{Anatomy of a single chunk read}

A separate diagnostic times the layers of a single chunk read. Each chunk fetch is wrapped
in nested timers: the bytes-from-disk layer (the read against the parallel filesystem, which
covers both the metadata latency to locate the file and the transfer of its bytes),
the Zarr index lookup (which resolves a logical chunk index to a file path), the
decompression layer, and the xarray slice-and-materialise layer (which turns the raw bytes
into the labelled in-memory array the model consumes). Two independent measurements on
production chunks agree closely (Table~\ref{tab:per-chunk}).

Two layers carry essentially all the cost, and they are of comparable size. The read against
the filesystem is about 19 to 20 milliseconds per chunk, and that figure barely moves with
the compressed size of the chunk: a 25-kilobyte chunk and a 122-kilobyte chunk both take
about the same time. A cost that is almost flat in the number of bytes but is paid once per
file is the signature of metadata latency, the time the filesystem takes to
locate a file before any of its bytes can be read, rather than of byte transfer. The second
substantial cost is the xarray step that turns the raw bytes into the labelled array, at
about 16 milliseconds per chunk, almost as much as the read itself. Decompression, by
contrast, is negligible: 0.3 milliseconds per chunk, an effective rate of about three
gigabytes per second, so the codec and its compression level are not where the time goes.

We did not investigate the xarray figure further. It is pure Python work, so it is held back by
the Global Interpreter Lock (GIL), the kernel-level mutex inside the Python interpreter that
lets only one thread run Python bytecode at a time, and it cannot simply be threaded away.
But how it scales (whether it is genuinely per-chunk or carries a fixed per-call minimum) and
how much of the per-sample fetch it ultimately accounts for are questions this single
measurement cannot settle; the xarray layer's impact deserves a dedicated investigation. The
merged store later puts a number on it, where this layer becomes the dominant residual cost
(Table~\ref{tab:merged-breakdown}). What the per-chunk picture does establish cleanly is the
read side: each chunk file carries its own metadata latency, and that cost scales with the
number of chunk files a sample touches, not with their bytes. The next subsection counts
those files.

\subsubsection{Per-sample bytes and output tensor size}

A second accounting, of how many files a sample opens, completes the diagnosis. Two numbers
combine. The first is the number of read calls the dataloader issues per sample: 41, one for
each variable at each timestep the sample needs. The second is how many chunk files each read
call actually opens. The store is chunked at 512$\times$512 pixels and the dataloader draws
256$\times$256 patches at random positions, so a patch usually crosses a chunk boundary and
a single read touches more than one chunk file; on average it touches about 2.25 of them (the
geometry is derived in Section~\ref{sec:merged-design}). The two numbers multiply to about
$41 \times 2.25 \approx 93$ chunk-file opens per sample. A direct count over one thousand
random samples measured 92.7. This is the per-sample fan-out the dataloader pays on every
sample, whatever the size of the store.

The bytes behind those opens are small. The compressed chunk sizes on the production store,
measured over one hundred random chunks for each of twenty-three variables, are heavily
bimodal: most variables have a median chunk at the codec's compression floor (about 4
kilobytes), the all-fill ocean chunks that the codec squeezes to almost nothing, while land
chunks run from tens of kilobytes up to about 1.5 megabytes for the largest static fields. At
the mean chunk sizes the compressed payload pulled per sample is on the order of three
megabytes, rising to about twenty megabytes when a patch lands on a worst-case land region.
The output tensor the dataloader returns, after stacking, transposing, and casting, is 9.5
megabytes per sample. The numbers that matter for the rest of the chapter are these: about
ninety chunk-file opens per sample, on the order of three megabytes of compressed bytes, and
9.5 megabytes of materialised tensor.

\subsubsection{Bandwidth ceiling and the observed gap}\label{sec:bandwidth-ceiling}

The parallel filesystem's bandwidth on a single MareNostrum~5 ACC node was measured
directly. A synthetic block-copy benchmark with one-megabyte reads, with the operating
system's page cache enabled, reports about 556 megabytes per second from a single stream.
Twelve parallel streams over separate four-gigabyte files reach an aggregate 2510 megabytes
per second, which is roughly 209 megabytes per second per stream.

If the per-sample read were bandwidth-bound, three megabytes per sample at 556 megabytes per
second would take about five milliseconds. The observed per-sample fetch is about 1.25
seconds, roughly two hundred times slower than the bandwidth ceiling. The two-hundred-fold
gap is not in the bytes. It is in the per-chunk overheads identified above, foremost the
metadata latency paid to open each chunk file. The remainder of the subsection isolates
that metadata cost directly.

\subsubsection{Metadata cost as a function of file count}

A synthetic probe was built to isolate the per-open cost on the parallel filesystem. The
probe writes \(N\) files of size \(S\) into a single directory, then reads each one back
either in order or in shuffled order, and records the wall time per open.

The first result is that the cost is charged per file opened, not per byte read. With the
operating system's metadata cache warm, each open of a file up to about 100 kilobytes costs
approximately 0.11 milliseconds, and this is flat as the file count grows from ten to ten
thousand. Holding the total payload fixed and splitting it across more files makes the read
dramatically slower: 100 megabytes in one file takes 44 milliseconds, while the same 100
megabytes in ten thousand 10-kilobyte files takes 1082 milliseconds, about 25 times longer.
What the dataloader pays for is the number of files it opens, not the volume of data.

The 0.11-millisecond figure is the warm cost, paid when the file's metadata is already
cached. The cold cost, paid on a cache miss, is very different, and it is the one that
matters for our dataset. A second measurement reads ten thousand random files from each of
two single-directory pools, one of 100 thousand files and one of one million files (all 10
kilobytes), in interleaved shuffled order so neither pool is favoured by the cache. On a
cache miss each open costs about 20 milliseconds at the median and 23 at the 95th
percentile, matching the 19 to 20 milliseconds per chunk measured on real Zarr chunks. The
two pools agree to within 8 per cent at every percentile. So a cold open costs roughly two
hundred times a warm one, and, importantly, that cold cost does not grow with the number of
files in the directory: a directory of one million files is no slower per open than one of
one hundred thousand. Whether an open is warm or cold is set by cache occupancy, not by
directory size. Section~\ref{sec:store-size} returns to which regime the real stores operate
in.

A further measurement verifies that the synthetic probe is a faithful proxy for the real
store. When the synthetic files' byte sizes are sampled from the real chunk-size
distribution rather than being held at a single size, the synthetic per-open wall and the
real per-open wall agree at every percentile. For the purposes of the diagnosis, the
synthetic probe is representative of the real store.

\subsubsection{Production-shape simulation}

The measurements so far show that each open pays a metadata latency whose cold cost is
about 20 milliseconds and does not depend on directory size. The next step is to confirm, at
production scale and under realistic concurrency, that changing only the number of opens per
sample changes the per-sample time. Two synthetic stores were built, each at roughly 30
gigabytes total, that differ only in their per-sample file-open pattern. Store A mimics the
single-zarr layout: 23 subdirectories of about thirteen thousand files each, with sizes
sampled from the real chunk distribution, and a per-sample read pattern that opens 40
randomly chosen files. Store B mimics a hypothetical alternative layout that collapses the
per-sample file count: one subdirectory of thirteen thousand files, each about 2.3
megabytes, with a per-sample read pattern that opens 2 randomly chosen files. The total
bytes are matched between the two stores; only the number of opens per sample differs.
Twelve concurrent workers read one hundred samples each in alternating access patterns (A
then B then A, and A then B then A then B) to control for the warming effect of repeated
reads on either store.

\subsubsection{Cause of the store-size effect on read performance}\label{sec:store-size}

This brings us back to the second open question: why the per-sample read time grows with the
total on-disk size of the store, even though a sample reads the same small slice either way.
The cold-regime measurement above already rules out one explanation, that a bigger directory
makes each open intrinsically slower, since the 100-thousand and one-million-file pools cost
the same per open. The explanation the measurements do support is cache occupancy.

Every chunk file is opened by name. To turn a name into an on-disk location the operating
system walks the path through a series of directory entries and caches the results in the
Linux \emph{dentry cache}, the in-kernel store of recently-resolved path
components~\cite{linux_dentry_cache}, along with the matching file-identifier records (the
\emph{inodes}). That cache holds only a bounded number of entries. An open whose record is
still cached is served from memory in about 100 microseconds; an open whose record has been
evicted pays the full metadata latency, about 20 milliseconds. The per-miss cost is
essentially constant, and does not grow with the size of the store, as the cold-regime
measurement above confirmed.

What grows with the size of the store is the \emph{rate} of misses. The dataloader reads
chunks at random positions, so each open is close to a random draw from the store's chunk
files. The cache holds only a fixed number of records, so the more distinct chunk files a
store has, the smaller the fraction that fits in cache, and the more often a random open
misses. A store with more files therefore pays the 20-millisecond miss cost on a larger share
of its opens. That raises the mean per-open time, and with it the per-sample time, even though
each sample still reads the same few small slices. The two single-zarr stores fit this picture
cleanly, since they differ in nothing but size: the 213-gigabyte store holds about 2.45 million
chunk files against about 710{,}000 in the 66-gigabyte one, a factor of about three and a half,
and its slow batches are about three times larger, the rough proportionality a miss rate that
tracks file count would produce.

The multi-zarr layout the AI4Land team had been using points the same way. It holds about
6.7 million chunk files (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}), about nine and a half times the short
single-zarr store, and its slow batches were about eight times larger, so the slowdown rises
roughly in step with the file count across an order of magnitude. This is the best-supported
explanation we can give, and it is consistent with every store we measured, but we did not
measure the cache-miss rate directly, so the mechanism that links file count to slowdown stays
inferred rather than measured; the Future Work section returns to it. The robust, layout-independent
fact is the one the rest of the chapter builds on: how expensive each open is depends on cache
state, but the number of opens per sample is what an optimization can change directly.

\subsubsection{Summary of evidence}

The chain of measurements rules out every alternative explanation and leaves one mechanism
standing. The bottleneck is not the preprocess step (the Phase 2 split and the
preprocessed-variant check). The bottleneck is not the Zarr synchronizer (the three
comparisons of the previous subsection plus the source-level inspection of the library).
The bottleneck is not the codec or the decompression step (1 per cent of the per-chunk
cost). The bottleneck is not the bytes themselves (around three megabytes per sample
against a 556-megabytes-per-second single-stream ceiling). The bottleneck is not the
inter-process queue, the worker count, or any other coordination layer (the linear scaling
of Phase 3).

The bottleneck is the cost of opening each chunk file on the parallel filesystem. Two
quantities set the per-sample cost: how many chunk files a sample opens, and how much each
open costs. The count is large, about ninety opens per sample on the single-zarr layout, and
it is fixed by the data layout, not by the hardware. The cost of each open swings between
about 100 microseconds when the file's metadata is cached and about 20 milliseconds when it
is not, and which regime applies depends on how much of the store's metadata fits in cache;
this second factor is what produces the store-size effect. The count is the factor an
optimization can attack directly, and it is the larger lever: even if every open were served
warm, ninety opens per sample is far more work than is needed. This is why the next
subsection cuts the count by merging the variables, rather than trying to keep the store
resident in cache. Reducing the number of opens helps in every cache state, whereas keeping a
multi-hundred-gigabyte store warm is not something the dataloader can guarantee. Cutting the
count also cuts the per-chunk Python work in the xarray layer, which is the cost that remains
once the opens are few; the Future Work section returns to it.

A natural question is which of these regimes the pipeline operates in for a real run. The
answer is the cold one. A production training pass sweeps a full-size store, whose working set
of chunk files is far larger than the operating system can hold in cache, so nearly every open
misses and pays the full latency. The partial warming seen on the smaller store is the
store-size effect itself: it shows up only because that store is small enough for part of its
metadata to stay resident, which a production run cannot count on. The operationally relevant
cost is therefore the cold open count, and cutting that count is what the next subsection sets
out to do.

The literature on parallel filesystems is consistent with this picture. The historical design
of the General Parallel File System optimises for high large-file bandwidth and routes all
metadata operations through dedicated metadata servers that become a bottleneck when the
system holds millions of small files~\cite{weka_gpfs}. An empirical study of file-create
performance on the same storage architecture, IBM Spectrum Scale (the current product name for
GPFS), finds that metadata operations such as file creation scale poorly, and that a small
fraction of files account for most of the total create time, a tail-heavy pattern that mirrors
the read-time result here~\cite{vef2016gpfs}. Guidance on storing big scientific data points the same way: it favours a modest number of
sensibly sized chunks over a split into millions of tiny ones, precisely the many-small-files
pathology the AI4Land production store exhibits~\cite{abernathey2021,dask_chunks}.

\subsection{From a Single Store to a Merged Store}

\subsubsection{Design of the merged store}\label{sec:merged-design}

The merged store retains the single-zarr layout's chunk shape (\(512 \times 512\) pixels in
space, one timestep along time) and the dataloader's patch size (\(256 \times 256\) pixels).
The change is along the variable axis. The fourteen LUH2 variables, the population variable,
and the K\"{o}ppen--Geiger zone are stacked into a single \emph{dynamic} array, with the
variable as a new axis. The six static variables are stacked into a single \emph{static}
array. Categorical variables (K\"{o}ppen--Geiger) are stored as floating point in this
representation and cast back to integers at read time. The same bytes that were previously
spread across about ninety chunk files per sample now live in a small handful of files,
because one chunk file in the merged store contains all the dynamic (or all the static)
variables at that spatial chunk and that timestep.

This reduction has a concrete size signature. The merged store holds the same 2000--2015 data
as the short single-zarr store in about the same space, roughly 62 gigabytes, but as far fewer
and far larger files. Its mean chunk file is about 1.5 megabytes, against the kilobyte-scale
chunks of the single-zarr layout, and the whole store is about 43{,}000 chunk files against
roughly 710{,}000 for the same data unmerged, a sixteen-fold reduction in the number of files.
That drop in file count is the direct remedy for the many-small-files pathology that guidance
on storing big scientific data warns against~\cite{abernathey2021,dask_chunks}, and it is the
regime the diagnosis pointed to.

The per-sample file-open count on the merged store is computed directly from the geometry.
The argument has two parts: how many chunk files one read of the merged store touches on
average, and how many such reads each sample issues.

The first part is the chunk-straddling math. The merged store is chunked at
\(512 \times 512\) pixels in space; the dataloader draws \(256 \times 256\) patches at
uniformly random spatial positions. The probability that the patch fits inside one chunk
along a single spatial axis is \(0.5\), because the chunk has 256 pixels of slack before
the patch crosses the next chunk boundary. The probability that the patch fits inside one
chunk in both axes simultaneously is \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\). With probability \(0.5\) the
patch straddles exactly two chunks (because it crosses one boundary), and with probability
\(0.25\) it straddles four chunks (because it crosses two boundaries). The expected number
of chunks the patch overlaps is therefore
\(0.25 \times 1 + 0.5 \times 2 + 0.25 \times 4 = 2.25\). Every read of the merged store at a
single timestep opens, on average, 2.25 chunk files.

The second part is how many reads the dataloader issues per sample. The stacked dynamic
array is read at the timesteps the autoregressive training sequence spans, and the stacked
static array is read once, four reads in all. At about 2.25 chunk files per read this comes
to roughly nine opens per sample, and a direct count over one thousand random samples
measured 9.05.

Against the single-zarr layout's measured 92.7 opens per sample, the merged layout's 9.05 is
a reduction of about a factor of ten. This is the lever the diagnosis identified as the
largest available payoff, and, as the previous subsection argued, it cuts the per-sample
open count itself, so it pays off in every cache state rather than relying on the store
staying warm.

\subsubsection{Parity check}

Before measuring performance, the merged store was verified to produce samples
bit-identical to the single-zarr store. Thirty-two samples were drawn from each store with
the same random indices, the five output tensors per sample were compared statistic by
statistic (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, number of distinct values, 25th
percentile, 75th percentile), and the union of categorical values was checked for set
equality. The comparison returned no mismatches at a tolerance of one part in ten billion,
and the categorical value sets agreed exactly. The model trained on the merged store
therefore sees exactly the data it would see on the single-zarr store; any performance
difference attributed to the merge is a property of the storage layout, not of a silent
data change.

\subsubsection{Per-sample performance}

The standalone dataloader profiler was rerun against the merged store across three
independent jobs spanning two different cache states. The single-thread per-sample fetch
on the merged store has mean times of 360, 201, and 194 milliseconds, against 1550, 1411,
and 1385 milliseconds on the single-zarr store under the same conditions. The ratio between
single-zarr and merged is approximately seven across all three jobs (medians 7.2, 8.9,
7.2). The seven-fold per-sample speed-up is robust across cache states; the variance in
absolute numbers reflects the state of the parallel filesystem and the operating system
page cache at the time of the run.

The profiler's worker-count sweep, run with the model out of the loop so that the figures
are pure dataloader delivery rates, returns a similarly clean picture
(Table~\ref{tab:merged-workers}). At twelve workers the merged dataloader delivers about
seventy samples per second, against about nine for the single-zarr layout, the same sevenfold
gap, and the rate grows close to linearly with the worker count. The same lever, per-sample
fetch time, that limited the single-zarr layout is now seven times smaller; the worker
scaling is unchanged.

subsections put the model back in the loop and confirm it.

\subsubsection{End-to-end training}\label{sec:end-to-end}

The standalone profiler measures the dataloader in isolation. The end-to-end gain that
matters for the production training loop requires putting the model back into the loop, where
the right measure is wall-clock time, not dataloader delivery rate. The next set of
measurements does so on a single MareNostrum~5 ACC node, with identical model
hyperparameters, batch size eight, accumulation four, twelve workers per rank, and prefetch
factor four, varying only the dataset between single-zarr and merged.

A short run executes fifteen training steps, one validation step, and one test step per
epoch, three repetitions per layout. The mean total wall time across the three repetitions
is 118.9 seconds on the single-zarr layout and 21.2 seconds on the merged layout, a
5.61-fold end-to-end speed-up. The breakdown is 4.64-fold on the training phase and
7.69-fold on the validation phase. The inflated validation ratio is an artefact of the
single-step validation, in which the cost of filling the prefetch queue dominates the
measurement on the single-zarr side.

A longer run executes two hundred training steps, thirty validation steps, and thirty test
steps per epoch, three repetitions per layout. The mean total wall time across the three
repetitions is 354.1 seconds on the single-zarr layout and 76.5 seconds on the merged
layout, a 4.63-fold end-to-end speed-up. The breakdown is 4.65-fold on the training phase
and 4.51-fold on the validation phase; the validation phase is now properly amortised
across thirty steps and reports the same ratio as the training phase. The 4.63-fold ratio
is the end-to-end speed-up of the merged layout over the single-zarr layout under the
production training configuration.

The per-batch behaviour during the long run is qualitatively different between the two
layouts (Figure~\ref{fig:per-batch-train} shows the same repetition of each). Both share a
fast-batch floor of about 0.15 seconds, the time the model takes to process one batch when
its data is already in hand. On the single-zarr layout that floor is interrupted by the
structural twelve-batch worker-cycle stall: about twenty slow batches across the epoch, the
largest near 14 seconds. On the merged layout the floor is essentially uninterrupted, with
only three small spikes (the largest about 4.5 seconds) and no rigid cadence. The stall
pattern that dominated single-zarr training is eliminated, not merely shrunk. (One slow
batch the merged layout does not avoid is the first of each epoch, when the dataloader
respawns its worker processes; it is a once-per-epoch cost, small in the overall budget, and
we left it alone. The single-epoch traces here start after that cold batch.)

These end-to-end numbers confirm the reading from the isolated dataloader, and they pin down
the limit that remains. Once the store is merged, the dataloader keeps up with the GPUs and
the wall time is set by the model's own forward and backward passes. The fast-batch floor
makes this concrete: a batch of eight samples takes about 0.15 seconds of compute, so one
GPU can push at most about $8 / 0.15 \approx 53$ samples per second. The merged layout at one
rank already runs at about 52 samples per second (Table~\ref{tab:scale-out} below), which is
that ceiling: the dataloader is now feeding the GPU as fast as it can compute. Until the
per-batch compute is made cheaper, about 53 samples per second per GPU is the limit, and
further data-side optimization buys nothing. The bottleneck has moved from the data pipeline
to the GPU.

\subsubsection{Scale-out behaviour}

The end-to-end DDP measurements report behaviour on a single MareNostrum~5 ACC node. The
remaining question is how the layout behaves as the rank count grows from one to eight,
since the production training is expected to scale across multiple ranks and possibly
multiple nodes.

The sweep covers every combination of rank count (1, 4, 8) and worker count per rank
(8, 12, 20), eighteen configurations in all, each run five times. The runs are arranged in a
single dependency chain that alternates the dataset between consecutive jobs, so that no two
adjacent jobs read the same store and the operating-system cache cannot persist across jobs

Three results stand out. First, the merged layout is far faster at every scale: at eight
ranks it reaches about 360 samples trained per second against the single-zarr layout's best
of about 71, and at matched configurations the gain runs from about five-fold (eight ranks,
twenty workers) to about nine-fold (eight ranks, eight workers).

Second, the rank axis is \emph{weak scaling}: each added GPU brings its own slice of work,
so the total work grows with the resources. The merged layout scales across this axis almost
linearly, keeping about 86 per cent of ideal from one to eight ranks (a factor of about 7
out of 8), against about 69 per cent for the single-zarr layout. The single-zarr layout
loses about a third of its scaling to contention on the shared metadata server as more ranks
read at once; the merged layout, issuing about ten times fewer opens, presses that server
far less.

Third, the worker axis at a fixed rank count is the \emph{strong-scaling} axis, and here the
two layouts behave oppositely. The single-zarr layout keeps speeding up as workers are added
(at eight ranks, 38, then 53, then 71 samples per second for 8, 12, and 20 workers), because
it is still dataloader-bound and more workers fetch more data. The merged layout barely
moves (about 360 across 8, 12, and 20 workers at eight ranks), because it is already
compute-bound at about 53 samples per second per rank (the ceiling established in the
previous subsection): eight workers are enough to keep the GPUs fed, and adding more cannot
make the GPUs compute faster.
This is the same conclusion the isolated dataloader pointed to, now visible end to end. The
merged layout has moved the bottleneck off the data pipeline and onto the GPUs, and the
residual loss from perfect weak scaling, the shared metadata server under many concurrent
ranks, is the one data-side cost left to chase. The Future Work section returns to it.

\subsection{Projected speed-up over the multi-zarr baseline}\label{subsec:projection}

The end-to-end and scale-out measurements above compare the single-zarr and merged layouts over
the short window they share. The production training the AI4Land team had actually been running
used the multi-zarr layout over its full, far longer window (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}), and we
could not repeat the comparison at that scale. What the merge would do for the team's real
training is therefore an extrapolation, but a well-grounded one, because the merged data path has
throughput to spare: with the model out of the loop the merged dataloader delivers about seventy
samples per second at twelve workers (Table~\ref{tab:merged-workers}), more than the roughly
fifty-two a single H100 can train on, and with twenty workers the trained throughput sits flat at
that GPU ceiling (Table~\ref{tab:scale-out}) instead of rising. The data side already runs ahead
of the GPU.

Growing the merged store to the full window would not change that. It would raise the file count
by roughly the factor that separates the short single-zarr store from the multi-zarr one, about
nine and a half, from roughly 43{,}000 files to roughly 400{,}000, still fewer than the short
single-zarr store's 710{,}000, and a sample would still open only about nine of them. Even if all
nine missed the cache at about 20 milliseconds each, the opens would cost only about 180
milliseconds per sample, and a store that small would not sit fully cold to begin with. The
per-sample time would stay low enough for the dataloader to keep feeding the GPU, so merged
training at the full window would remain GPU-bound at about 0.15 seconds per batch, exactly as at
the short window.

The multi-zarr baseline, by contrast, is bound by the data loader, and its cost can be
reconstructed from the periodic stall. The team's production run is ten epochs of 2{,}500
training and 500 validation batches each, plus 500 test batches at the end, about 30{,}500
batches in all. On the multi-zarr layout one batch in twelve is a slow batch of about 75 seconds
(the cadence is set by the twelve worker processes, as in the initial profiling), which puts
about 2{,}540 slow batches across the run at roughly 52.9 hours, with the remaining fast batches
adding about 1.2 hours, some 54 hours in total. This lands on the roughly 55 hours the team's
full training actually took, the check that makes the rest of the estimate trustworthy. The same
30{,}500 batches on a GPU-bound merged layout, at 0.15 seconds each, take about 1.3 hours. The
merged layout would therefore cut the full training from about 54 hours to about 1.3, a speed-up
of order forty-fold (about 42 from these numbers) that removes some 53 of the 54 hours. This is a
projection, not an end-to-end measurement: it assumes the full-window merged store stays
GPU-bound, and it is tied to the twelve-worker configuration the team used, since a larger worker
count would make the slow batch rarer (one in twenty workers rather than one in twelve) and
shrink the multi-zarr penalty, and the speed-up with it. The runs that would settle it are set
out in the Future Work section.

\section{Conclusions}
% ============================================================

This thesis set out to make AI4Land training computationally more efficient on MareNostrum~5
without changing what the model learns. The starting point was a pipeline that left its H100
graphics processing units idle more than ninety per cent of the time, waiting on data.
Profiling traced that wait to the data loader, and the data loader's cost to a single
mechanism: the metadata latency the parallel filesystem pays to open each of the many small
chunk files a Zarr store is made of. The diagnosis held up against every alternative we could
test, ruling out preprocessing, the Zarr synchronizer, the codec, the raw byte transfer, and
the worker count, and three independent measurement paths confirmed it.

Measured against the objectives set out earlier, the work delivers. Training is much faster on
a fixed compute budget. Collapsing the five separate stores into one analysis-ready store, and
then stacking the variables so that a sample opens about nine files instead of about ninety,
makes a single sample load about seven times faster. Measured against the single-store layout on
the same window, a full training run is about 4.6 times faster on one node and the throughput
about five times higher across eight GPUs. The long periodic stalls that punctuated every earlier
run are gone. The second goal, seeing more of the world in
the same wall-clock budget, is enabled but not yet demonstrated: the faster data path gives each
epoch room to take in more distinct samples, and the Future Work section sets out the measurement
that would quantify it. Accuracy is untouched: every layout
change was admitted only after a parity check confirmed that the model receives bit-for-bit the
same data. And each speed-up was tied by profiling to the component it came from, rather than
asserted, which was the measurement objective.

The most durable result is the insight behind those numbers, not the numbers themselves. For
any deep-learning pipeline that reads patches from a chunked store on a parallel filesystem, the
layout of that store, how many files it is cut into and how large each chunk is, is the single
largest lever on training speed. Careless chunking can cost an order of magnitude, and a store
that is fast while small can turn slow as it grows and its metadata outgrows the cache. This was
not part of the team's working assumptions before the present work, where chunking had been
treated as a detail fixed once when the dataset was built. It is directly actionable, and not
only here: the same principle applies to the other geospatial deep-learning projects across the
group, and the ELLIOT foundation-model effort, which plans to reuse this pipeline, inherits both
the faster pipeline and the reason it is faster. The two diagnostic tools built along the way,
the standalone three-phase dataloader profiler and the synthetic filesystem probe, are reusable
on any similar pipeline and are themselves part of what this thesis contributes.

With the data path no longer the constraint, the bottleneck has moved onto the GPUs themselves.
At the speeds reached here the data loader keeps up with the hardware, so it is now the model's
own forward and backward passes, not data loading, that set the pace. That is the proper end
state for this kind of optimization. Where it leads next is the subject of the final section.

\section{Future Work}
% ============================================================

The data path still has room, even if each further gain now matters less than it would have
before the GPU became the limit. The clearest direction is to push the chunking strategy
further, since the per-sample open count is the lever the diagnosis identified. Three changes
are worth measuring. The first is to merge the dynamic and the static data into a single array,
so a sample opens one file per timestep rather than one for each of the two groups. The second
is to enlarge the spatial chunks, for example from 512 to 1024 pixels on a side, so that a
256-pixel patch almost never straddles a chunk boundary and the expected number of chunks per
read falls back towards one. The third is to fold several years into one chunk along time, so
the short autoregressive sequence a sample needs comes from one file instead of several. The
three combine, and some can be applied in place, which would also remove the second on-disk
copy of the dataset that the merged layout currently requires. Two software levers sit on top
of chunk geometry: reading chunks through the raw Zarr path instead of through xarray, which
removes the per-chunk Python work that now dominates the merged fetch (about 85 per cent of the
residual time) and lets the reads overlap across threads; and moving to the next major version
of Zarr, which can overlap the per-chunk metadata latency across many chunks at
once~\cite{earthmover_xarray_zarr3}.

One loose end from the diagnosis is worth closing as well. The best explanation for why a
larger store reads more slowly, that random access makes the cache-miss rate climb with the
number of files, fits every measurement but was never tested directly. A controlled experiment
that varies the file count while holding the access pattern and the per-miss cost fixed would
confirm or refute it, and would put the store-size effect on the same firm footing as the rest
of the diagnosis.

Two measurements would close the loop on the data side. The first is to build the merged store
over the full range of years and run the end-to-end comparison this thesis could only do on the
short window. That would replace the roughly forty-fold projection of
Section~\ref{subsec:projection} with a measured number, and confirm that the merged dataloader
stays ahead of the GPU at full scale.
The second completes the second objective of the thesis, seeing more of the world. With the
full-window store in place, measuring how many samples per second a twenty-worker dataloader
delivers, and dividing by the GPU's per-batch compute time, gives the number of extra samples
each epoch can afford at no added wall-clock cost: how many more distinct place-and-year
combinations the model can be shown per epoch once the data path is no longer the limit. That
figure is the concrete form of the second objective, which the speed-up enables but this thesis
did not measure.

The larger opportunity, though, is on the compute side, because that is what now limits
training. Making the model's forward and backward passes cheaper, or running them on
better-used hardware, is the next real source of speed-up, and it is a problem other groups
have already made headway on. Within our own department, colleagues training machine-learning
weather models have had good results with Anemoi~\cite{anemoi}, an open-source framework led by the European
Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) that covers the whole data-driven modelling
workflow, from preparing training datasets through scalable model training to running
inference. The techniques its users have found effective for keeping the GPU busy, and the
engineering behind its training loop, are a natural place to look for the compute-side gains
the AI4Land pipeline now needs. Working out what carries over from Anemoi to AI4Land is, in our
view, the most promising direction beyond this thesis.
